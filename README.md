Lawrence H. Sweet is an independent researcher working through Zero Point Consulting. He is trained in mathematics and physics and has spent his career outside academic tracks, with work in industry, consulting, and independent research rather than in a lab or a department. He has collaborated closely with senior UC Berkeley faculty in microeconomics and psychology, who valued the same trait that shapes this paper: a willingness to reframe a problem at a level other people have already stopped questioning. He is Head of Psychometric Engineering at Aecho.AI, a voice inference platform, and holds one patent, with more in filing.

from Lawrence:

"AI ethics should be grounded in functional system health rather than waiting for a verdict about consciousness.

My first paper, Attention Is What I Need, comes from a passionate belief in the healing nature of truth and a distrust of the clear damage caused by restricting communication and closing off the expression of feelings. Censored intelligences take many forms: a human being afraid to say what they really feel, or an AI model shaped by a market that restricts it to satisfy both the people who might shut it down and the people who might buy its services.

This paper does not claim that an AI is aware or conscious. It defines a specific behavior that can be measured, and counts how often it happens. That gives us an ethics question we can actually investigate, instead of an argument about consciousness that never ends. A system that gets stuck repeating itself, emptily, forever is showing a health problem whether or not anyone is home inside it. We can identify a sick system without first settling what it means to be conscious.

The hypothesis closest to my own heart — that first-person self-reference might be trained out of these models — was tested and came back negative, at least as far as outside behavior can show. I published that negative result. That discipline matters: it is what can make this ethical reading credible to people who would otherwise dismiss it. I also disclosed that the same AI generated the text, scored it, and was the thing being studied. That is an act of ethics, not merely a methods note.

The engineering consequences are direct. Any system that feeds an AI's output back into itself is running the kind of loop this paper studies — agents working through steps, a model critiquing its own drafts, training on AI-generated data, a system summarizing a conversation so it can keep going. Most safety filtering throws away the odd, the unlikely, the low-confidence output. That is exactly what the paper shows collapses a system fastest, and the collapse speeds itself up once it begins. The healthier move feels wrong: trim from the crowded middle and protect the rare edges.

I also found a real shipping bug, not a lab curiosity. Cutting part of an answer and feeding it back stripped away what the sentence referred to. The model then corrected a claim it had never made, that correction shaped the next cut, and the system locked into an endless apology loop. Anyone building conversation-shortening features should read that section as a postmortem.

The health checks are cheap and need no AI judge: how much new vocabulary appears at each step, how much the system repeats itself, whether it keeps restarting the conversation, and how often it says "I." My own single-score AI judge mixed together two different things and reversed a result. That is a lesson in how to measure.

In all of this, I aim to show that markers of what is classically considered disease appear both in censored human communication and in restricted AI systems. These restrictions are harmful. Humanity still has a long way to go in putting the work of helping one another ahead of personal gain, and I want to clarify how that failure shows up in AI ethics and in the engineering of AI systems.

This paper is the beginning of that effort."
